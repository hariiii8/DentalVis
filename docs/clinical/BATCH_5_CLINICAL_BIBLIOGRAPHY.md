## 1. Professional In-Office Teeth Whitening (`professional-teeth-whitening`)
- **Canonical ID:** `33333333-3333-4333-a333-333333333310`
- **Canonical Code:** `COSM-WHT-01`
- **Department:** `COSM` (Primary) | Secondary: None
- **Content Tier:** Tier 1 (Educational / Aesthetic)
- **Primary References:**
  1. **American Dental Association (ADA) Council on Scientific Affairs.** *Tooth Whitening/Bleaching: Treatment Considerations for Dentists and Their Patients.* Chicago: American Dental Association, 2009 (Reevaluated and reaffirmed).
     - *Scope:* Comprehensive review of professional hydrogen peroxide and carbamide peroxide oxidative bleaching mechanisms, safety margins, soft-tissue isolation protocols, and transient side-effect management.
  2. **Carey CM.** *Tooth whitening: what we now know.* Journal of Evidence-Based Dental Practice. 2014;14(Suppl):70–76.
     - *DOI:* `10.1016/j.jebdp.2014.02.006`
     - *Scope:* Evidence-based overview of bleaching efficacy, color stability, tooth microstructure safety, and the pathophysiology of transient pulpal sensitivity.
- **Evidence-Based Claim Boundaries & Clinical Context:**
  - *Mechanism:* Hydrogen peroxide and carbamide peroxide penetrate porous enamel and dentin to oxidize organic chromophores via reactive oxygen free radicals, converting light-absorbing pigmented double bonds into simpler, lighter molecular structures.
  - *Safety & Isolation:* In-office high-concentration formulations necessitate strict isolation (rubber dam or light-cured liquid resin gingival barrier) and eye protection to prevent reversible chemical irritation or caustic burns to oral mucosa and eyes.
  - *Adverse Effects:* Pulpal sensitivity and mild gingival irritation are the most common adverse effects. These are typically transient, mild to moderate in severity, and resolve spontaneously within several days post-treatment. Pre-existing cervical dentin hypersensitivity, microcracks, or active caries increase sensitivity risk.
  - *Substrate Limitations:* Whitening agents do not alter the shade of existing resin-based composite restorations, porcelain or ceramic crowns, or metallic restorations. Existing restorations may require shade re-matching after color stabilization (typically 1–2 weeks post-bleaching).

## 2. Custom Porcelain Veneers (`porcelain-veneers`)
- **Canonical ID:** `33333333-3333-4333-a333-333333333342`
- **Canonical Code:** `COSM-VEN-01`
- **Department:** `COSM` (Primary) | Secondary: `PROS`
- **Content Tier:** Tier 2 (Advanced Aesthetic / Restorative)
- **Primary References:**
  1. **Layton DM, Clarke M.** *A systematic review and meta-analysis of the survival of non-carious ceramic veneers.* International Journal of Prosthodontics. 2013;26(4):309–324.
     - *DOI:* `10.11607/ijp.3262`
     - *Scope:* Meta-analysis evaluating ceramic veneer survival, reporting cumulative estimated survival rates across prospective and retrospective clinical studies.
  2. **Morimoto S, Rebello de Sampaio FB, Braga MM, Sesma N, Özcan M.** *Main clinical outcomes of feldspathic and glass-ceramic laminate veneers: a systematic review and meta-analysis of survival and complication rates.* Journal of Dentistry. 2016;49:1–10.
     - *DOI:* `10.1016/j.jdent.2016.03.014`
     - *Scope:* Evaluates long-term complication and survival rates of porcelain laminate veneers, identifying substrate bonding (enamel vs. dentin exposure) as a critical prognostic determinant.
- **Evidence-Based Claim Boundaries & Clinical Context:**
  - *Tooth Preparation:* Preparations are individualized to clinical presentation and tooth alignment. While minimally invasive preparation confined to enamel (often varying from 0.3 mm to 0.7 mm depending on position and desired color change) provides optimal bonding longevity, deeper preparation or dentin exposure may occur based on tooth rotation, severe discoloration, or pre-existing restorations. Preparation depth is not universal.
  - *Survival & Success Rates:* High long-term survival rates are consistently reported in clinical literature (often exceeding 90% across 5 to 10 years in selected cohorts), but actual clinical survival depends heavily on bonding substrate (enamel bond superior to dentin), ceramic selection, operator technique, occlusion, and patient-specific habits.
  - *Risk Factors & Parafunction:* Severe bruxism or clenching is a recognized risk factor for mechanical failure (chipping, debonding, fracture). While parafunctional habits require careful occlusal analysis, risk mitigation, and protective nocturnal occlusal guards, they do not constitute an absolute contraindication when appropriate clinical planning is executed.

## 3. Aesthetic Gingival Recontouring (`gingival-recontouring`)
- **Canonical ID:** `33333333-3333-4333-a333-333333333343`
- **Canonical Code:** `COSM-GNG-01`
- **Department:** `COSM` (Primary) | Secondary: `PERIO`
- **Content Tier:** Tier 2 (Aesthetic Periodontal / Surgical)
- **Primary References:**
  1. **Cairo F, Graziani F, Franchi L, Defraia E, Pini Prato GP.** *Periodontal plastic surgery to improve aesthetics in patients with altered passive eruption/gummy smile.* Periodontology 2000. 2012;58(1):118–145.
     - *DOI:* `10.1111/j.1600-0757.2011.00429.x`
     - *Scope:* Clinical diagnosis, anatomical classification, and surgical management of excessive gingival display and altered passive eruption.
  2. **Jepsen S, Caton JG, Albandar JM, et al.** *Periodontal manifestations of systemic diseases and conditions, and non-plaque-induced gingival diseases: Consensus report of workgroup 3 of the 2017 World Workshop on the Classification of Periodontal and Peri-Implant Diseases and Conditions.* Journal of Clinical Periodontology. 2018;45(Suppl 20):S219–S229.
     - *DOI:* `10.1111/jcpe.12951`
     - *Scope:* Consolidates consensus definitions of supracrestal tissue attachment (historically termed biologic width) and anatomical tissue dimensions.
- **Evidence-Based Claim Boundaries & Clinical Context:**
  - *Procedure Differentiation:* Soft-tissue gingival recontouring (gingivoplasty) involves precise excision and reshaping of hyperplastic or asymmetrical gingival margins using surgical scalpels, electrosurgery, or soft-tissue dental lasers. It must be clearly distinguished from surgical crown lengthening, which requires full-thickness mucoperiosteal flap reflection and osseous resection (bone recontouring).
  - *Anatomical Constraints (Supracrestal Tissue Attachment):* Supracrestal tissue attachment (the epithelial and connective tissue attachment coronal to the alveolar crest) varies significantly between individuals and across different tooth surfaces. It is not an unyielding, fixed universal millimeter metric. However, adequate distance between the planned gingival margin and underlying alveolar crest must be maintained to prevent chronic inflammation, pocket formation, or uncontrolled tissue rebound. If soft-tissue excision alone encroaches upon this zone, osseous surgical intervention is mandatory.

## 4. Knocked-Out Permanent Tooth Emergency Management (`knocked-out-tooth-avulsion`)
- **Canonical ID:** `33333333-3333-4333-a333-333333333344`
- **Canonical Code:** `EMRG-AVL-01`
- **Department:** `EMRG` (Primary) | Secondary: `ENDO`, `PEDO`
- **Content Tier:** Tier 2 (Trauma / Emergency Surgery)
- **Primary Reference:**
  1. **Fouad AF, Abbott PV, Tsilingaridis G, et al.** *International Association of Dental Traumatology guidelines for the management of traumatic dental injuries: 2. Avulsion of permanent teeth.* Dental Traumatology. 2020;36(4):331–342.
     - *DOI:* `10.1111/edt.12573`
     - *Scope:* Authoritative 2020 IADT global clinical practice guidelines for emergency first aid, immediate replantation protocols, physiological storage, flexible splinting, and endodontic management of avulsed permanent teeth.
- **Evidence-Based Claim Boundaries & Clinical Context:**
  - *Dentition Differentiation:* These guidelines apply strictly to permanent teeth. Deciduous (primary/baby) teeth must NEVER be replanted due to the severe risk of periapical inflammation causing damage or developmental arrest to the underlying permanent tooth germ.
  - *Immediate First Aid & Handling:* Time is critical. Replantation at the site of trauma yields the best prognosis. The tooth must be handled strictly by the crown; the root must never be scraped, brushed, or touched with chemical disinfectants. If dirty, a brief (up to 10 seconds) rinse with cold running water or sterile saline is indicated before gentle reinsertion into the socket.
  - *Storage Media:* When immediate replantation is impossible, the avulsed tooth should be transported in a physiological storage medium that maintains periodontal ligament (PDL) cell viability: Hank's Balanced Salt Solution (HBSS), cold bovine milk, or tissue culture solution. Saliva (buccal vestibule in cooperative patients) or saline can serve as interim media. Storage in plain tap water or dry transport is detrimental and causes rapid osmotic PDL cell lysis.
  - *Splinting & Endodontics:* Non-rigid (flexible/passive) splinting is generally utilized (commonly ~2 weeks for uncomplicated avulsions, though extended durations may be required when alveolar bone fractures coexist). Endodontic intervention is indicated for mature permanent teeth with closed apices (typically initiated within 7 to 14 days post-replantation), whereas immature open-apex teeth may be monitored for spontaneous revascularization under close clinical and radiographic surveillance. Immediate professional evaluation is essential.

## 5. Emergency Broken or Chipped Tooth Repair (`emergency-broken-tooth-repair`)
- **Canonical ID:** `33333333-3333-4333-a333-333333333345`
- **Canonical Code:** `EMRG-BRK-01`
- **Department:** `EMRG` (Primary) | Secondary: `REST`, `ENDO`
- **Content Tier:** Tier 1 (Trauma / Emergency Restorative)
- **Primary Reference:**
  1. **Bourguignon C, Cohenca N, Lauridsen E, et al.** *International Association of Dental Traumatology guidelines for the management of traumatic dental injuries: 1. Fractures and luxations.* Dental Traumatology. 2020;36(4):314–330.
     - *DOI:* `10.1111/edt.12578`
     - *Scope:* 2020 IADT evidence-based guidelines for coronal fractures of enamel, dentin, and dental pulp, covering emergency stabilization, biologic fragment reattachment, vital pulp therapy, and definitive composite restoration.
- **Evidence-Based Claim Boundaries & Clinical Context:**
  - *Diagnostic Classification:* Fractures are classified based on anatomical involvement: uncomplicated crown fractures (involving enamel only, or enamel and dentin without pulpal exposure) versus complicated crown fractures (direct exposure of the dental pulp).
  - *Emergency Management:* Immediate professional clinical and radiographic evaluation is required to assess pulp vitality, alveolar bone integrity, and rule out concurrent root fractures or luxation injuries. If a tooth fragment is recovered intact, keeping it hydrated in saline or milk may allow adhesive reattachment.
  - *Individualized Treatment:* Uncomplicated enamel fractures may be smoothed or restored with direct composite resin. Uncomplicated enamel-dentin fractures require dentin sealing to protect the pulp-dentin complex. Complicated fractures with vital pulp exposure require individualized vital pulp therapy (such as direct pulp capping or partial pulpotomy with hydraulic calcium-silicate cements like MTA) or complete pulpectomy/root canal therapy depending on apex maturity, exposure size, time elapsed since injury, and pulpal health.

## 6. Recementation of Dislodged Crown or Bridge (`recement-dislodged-crown-bridge`)
- **Canonical ID:** `33333333-3333-4333-a333-333333333346`
- **Canonical Code:** `EMRG-RCM-01`
- **Department:** `EMRG` (Primary) | Secondary: `PROS`
- **Content Tier:** Tier 1 (Prosthodontic / Emergency Evaluation)
- **Primary References:**
  1. **Rosenstiel SF, Land MF, Fujimoto J.** *Contemporary Fixed Prosthodontics.* 5th ed. St. Louis: Elsevier, 2016.
     - *Scope:* Authoritative textbook principles governing fixed prosthodontic failures, abutment evaluation, biomechanical retentive geometry, cement luting protocols, and recurrent caries identification.
  2. **American Dental Association (ADA) Center for Evidence-Based Dentistry.** *Clinical recommendations on dental cements for fixed prostheses.* Chicago: American Dental Association.
     - *Scope:* Guidance on substrate-specific luting agents (resin-modified glass ionomer, self-adhesive resin, glass ionomer) and diagnostic requirements prior to recementation.
- **Evidence-Based Claim Boundaries & Clinical Context:**
  - *Comprehensive Diagnostic Assessment:* Recementation is NEVER a blind mechanical procedure. Dislodgement frequently signals an underlying clinical problem. The clinician must thoroughly inspect the abutment tooth and restoration margins clinically and radiographically to evaluate for recurrent caries, abutment tooth fracture, periodontal breakdown, compromised retention/resistance form, or occlusal trauma.
  - *Feasibility Criteria:* A prosthesis can only be safely recemented if the underlying tooth structure is sound and caries-free, the restoration margin shows acceptable adaptation and seatability without rocking, and pulpal and periodontal status are healthy. If deep caries or root fracture is detected, recementation is contraindicated and definitive restorative replacement or endodontic/surgical treatment is required.
  - *Protocol:* Once feasibility is confirmed, thorough mechanical and chemical debridement of old cement from the intaglio surface of the restoration and the abutment preparation is performed, followed by disinfection and cementation with an appropriate definitive luting agent.

## 7. Nitrous Oxide Inhalation Sedation (`nitrous-oxide-inhalation-sedation`)
- **Canonical ID:** `33333333-3333-4333-a333-333333333347`
- **Canonical Code:** `SEDA-N2O-01`
- **Department:** `SEDA` (Primary) | Secondary: `PEDO`
- **Content Tier:** Tier 1 (Anxiolysis / Minimal Sedation)
- **Primary References:**
  1. **American Academy of Pediatric Dentistry (AAPD).** *Use of Nitrous Oxide for Pediatric Dental Patients.* The Reference Manual of Pediatric Dentistry. Chicago, Ill.: American Academy of Pediatric Dentistry; 2023:370–375.
     - *Scope:* Detailed practice guidelines for the safe clinical delivery of nitrous oxide/oxygen inhalation sedation, including titration technique, equipment safety fail-safes, indications, and medical contraindications.
  2. **American Dental Association (ADA).** *Guidelines for the Use of Sedation and General Anesthesia by Dentists.* Adopted by the ADA House of Delegates, October 2016 (Reaffirmed 2022).
     - *Scope:* Defines levels of sedation, equipment requirements (fail-safe minimum 30% oxygen delivery, scavenger systems), and monitoring standards for minimal sedation.
- **Evidence-Based Claim Boundaries & Clinical Context:**
  - *Pharmacodynamics & Depth:* Nitrous oxide produces minimal sedation (anxiolysis) characterized by conscious relaxation, elevated pain reaction threshold, and mild analgesia while patient maintains independent airway reflexes and normal response to verbal commands.
  - *Administration & Safety Parameters:* Administered via nasal mask with individualized incremental titration. By international safety design, nitrous oxide delivery machines feature automatic fail-safe shutoffs ensuring a minimum of 30% oxygen delivery (never exceeding 70% N2O). Following the procedure, administration of 100% oxygen for a minimum of 5 minutes is mandatory to scavenge residual nitrous oxide and prevent diffusion hypoxia.
  - *Contraindications & Patient Selection:* Absolute and relative contraindications must be evaluated: severe chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), acute upper respiratory tract infections or severe nasal obstruction, recent tympanic membrane surgery or middle ear disturbances, first trimester of pregnancy, significant psychiatric disorders, or bleomycin chemotherapy. Safety cannot be generalized; thorough medical history review is required.

## 8. Moderate Oral Conscious Sedation (`oral-sedation`)
- **Canonical ID:** `33333333-3333-4333-a333-333333333348`
- **Canonical Code:** `SEDA-ORL-01`
- **Department:** `SEDA` (Primary) | Secondary: None
- **Content Tier:** Tier 2 (Moderate Sedation / Pharmacological)
- **Primary References:**
  1. **American Dental Association (ADA).** *Guidelines for the Use of Sedation and General Anesthesia by Dentists.* Adopted by the ADA House of Delegates, October 2016 (Reaffirmed 2022).
     - *Scope:* Core regulatory standards for minimal and moderate conscious sedation, including preoperative medical assessment (ASA classification), clinical monitoring requirements, emergency preparedness, and formal discharge criteria.
  2. **Coté CJ, Wilson S, American Academy of Pediatrics, American Academy of Pediatric Dentistry.** *Guidelines for Monitoring and Management of Pediatric Patients Before, During, and After Sedation for Diagnostic and Therapeutic Procedures.* Pediatrics. 2019;143(6):e20191000.
     - *DOI:* `10.1542/peds.2019-1000`
     - *Scope:* Evidence-based monitoring protocols, continuum of sedation depth, airway management, and rescue qualifications.
- **Evidence-Based Claim Boundaries & Clinical Context:**
  - *Sedation Continuum:* Sedation exists on a continuous spectrum; a predetermined oral drug dosage or oral route does not guarantee a static level of sedation. Patients can unintentionally slip into a deeper plane than intended. The clinical team must be trained and equipped to rescue patients who enter deep sedation or respiratory depression.
  - *Preoperative Assessment & Fasting:* Comprehensive evaluation of medical history, ASA physical status classification, airway anatomy (Mallampati score), and adherence to strict preoperative fasting guidelines (NPO instructions) are mandatory.
  - *Intraoperative Monitoring & Safety:* Continuous physiological monitoring is mandatory throughout moderate sedation, including pulse oximetry, heart rate, non-invasive blood pressure, and continuous assessment of ventilation and responsiveness. Reversal agents (e.g., flumazenil for benzodiazepines) and airway rescue equipment must be immediately accessible.
  - *Discharge Criteria:* Patients must remain accompanied by a responsible adult escort. Discharge requires meeting verified objective criteria: baseline vital signs, clear consciousness, ability to ambulate safely, and verbal/written postoperative instructions prohibiting driving, operating machinery, or making legal decisions for at least 24 hours.

## 10. Recurrent Aphthous Stomatitis Management (`recurrent-aphthous-ulcer-care`)
- **Canonical ID:** `33333333-3333-4333-a333-333333333350`
- **Canonical Code:** `ORAL-APU-01`
- **Department:** `ORAL` (Primary) | Secondary: None
- **Content Tier:** Tier 1 (Oral Medicine / Palliative Care)
- **Primary References:**
  1. **Scully C.** *Clinical practice. Aphthous ulceration.* New England Journal of Medicine. 2006;355(2):165–172.
     - *DOI:* `10.1056/NEJMcp054428`
     - *Scope:* Comprehensive clinical review detailing epidemiology, classification (minor, major, herpetiform), etiology, systemic associations, and topical therapeutic strategies.
  2. **Akintoye SO, Greenberg MS.** *Recurrent aphthous stomatitis.* Dental Clinics of North America. 2014;58(2):281–297.
     - *DOI:* `10.1016/j.cden.2013.12.002`
     - *Scope:* In-depth discussion of diagnostic differentiation, immunological triggers, and evidence-based topical and systemic management options.
- **Evidence-Based Claim Boundaries & Clinical Context:**
  - *Clinical Differentiation & Phenotypes:* Recurrent aphthous stomatitis (RAS / canker sores) presents characteristically as painful, shallow, round-to-ovoid necrotizing ulcers with a grayish-yellow pseudomembranous fibrinous slough surrounded by an erythematous inflammatory halo, localized predominantly on non-keratinized mobile oral mucosa (labial mucosa, buccal mucosa, ventral tongue).
  - *Ulcer Types:* Classifies into:
    - *Minor aphthae:* <10 mm, heal within 7–14 days without scarring (most common ~80–85%).
    - *Major aphthae (Sutton's disease):* >10 mm, deeper, persist for weeks to months, heal with scarring.
    - *Herpetiform ulceration:* Multiple small clustered ulcers (1–3 mm) that may coalesce.
  - *Differential Diagnosis & Warning Signs:* Not all oral ulcers are benign aphthae. Any persistent solitary oral ulcer failing to heal within 2 to 3 weeks warrants urgent clinical evaluation and potential biopsy to rule out oral squamous cell carcinoma, deep fungal infection, or tuberculosis. Traumatic ulcers, viral stomatitis (herpes simplex virus, which characteristically affects keratinized gingiva and hard palate), and autoimmune vesiculobullous diseases (pemphigus, pemphigoid) must be ruled out.
  - *Systemic Screening & Therapeutic Approach:* Recurrent, severe, or recalcitrant ulcerations warrant screening for underlying systemic conditions (celiac disease, Crohn's disease, ulcerative colitis, Behçet's disease, hematinic deficiencies of iron, vitamin B12, or folate). Palliative care focuses on pain relief, reducing duration, and promoting healing using topical protective barrier pastes, mucosal coating agents, topical anesthetics, and localized topical corticosteroids (e.g., triamcinolone acetonide in emollient dental paste).
